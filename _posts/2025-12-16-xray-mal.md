As long as there have been coins, there have been forgers.  A corollary to this fact is that any collection of sufficient size will contain some fakes. Many ancient fakes used a false core of copper with a silver or gold casing to pass as an authentic coin[^1]. These [fourrées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourr%C3%A9e) (French for 'stuffed') are often hard to visually distinguish from authentic coins given the skill of the forgers or their illicit use of official dies. In ancient times this led to the use of [test cuts](https://www.forumancientcoins.com/numiswiki//view.asp?key=Test%20Cut) to physically open or cut coins to ensure their inner metal quality.

To verify the authenticity of our collection, the Madison Art Collection has often partnered with the [Madison Accelerator Lab](https://sites.lib.jmu.edu/mal/) to X-ray objects to verify their inner structure. When applied to coins, fourrees and authentic coins will "respond" differently under X-ray as they have differing material composition. Objects made of lighter atomic mass, such as copper, will permit x-rays to pass more easily as compared to materials with heavier atomic mass, such as silver. Applying this principle to coin analysis, if two objects are roughly the same size and weight, an object that is mostly copper, such as a fourree, will "disappear" more quickly under x-ray imaging than objects of solid silver.

We can see this behavior dramatically on the right in [Figure 1](#xray-figure). The modern US coins on the left (mostly made of copper, tin, or light amounts of silver), and the known fourrees on the right, rapidly "disappear" as the energy level of x-rays increases. However, the "good" denarii in the middle last longer and remain until the x-rays reach their maximum energy levels. 

The results from this experiment are clear. The known fakes, [Didrachm of Cales](https://jmu.emuseum.com/objects/1345/didrachm-fourree-of-cales-campania?ctx=662334f6726c8baf0e59938e980d74aa9d680e67&idx=0) (item D) and [Denarius of Augustus](https://jmu.emuseum.com/objects/8792/denarius-fourree-of-augustus-p-petronius-turpilianus-as?ctx=35390601737cbe164b79324a8f3a347d7ebd0af5&idx=2) (item F), were re-confirmed to be fake. However, a suspected [coin of Augustus](https://jmu.emuseum.com/objects/8802/denarius-of-augustus-l-vinicius-as-moneyer?ctx=2344bf491cfa21f8779c6093bdc043fc6f544dd8&idx=0) (item E), was verified as authentic! 

The Madison Art Collection is grateful for the assistance of [Dr. Harold Butner](https://www.jmu.edu/physics/people/butner-harold.shtml), Professor of Physics, in conducting these experiments and [Dr. Adriana Banu](https://www.jmu.edu/physics/people/banu-adriana.shtml), Professor of Physics and Scientific Director of the Madison Accelerator Lab, for continued access to this crucial equipment.
